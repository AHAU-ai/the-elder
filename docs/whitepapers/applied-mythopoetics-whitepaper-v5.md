## §1. The Field: What Mythopoetics Names

The term mythopoetics is far older than the twentieth century. It descends from the Hellenistic Greek *muthopoiía* (μυθοποιία) — "myth-making," from *mythos* (μῦθος) + *poiein* (ποιεῖν, "to make"). The word already carries the tension it would carry for two thousand years: in Plato's *Phaedo*, the mythos/logos distinction sets story-making against reasoned argument, and later Hellenistic writers — Strabo, Diodorus Siculus — used *muthopoiía* as a term of diagnosis, a way of flagging material as invented, unreliable, merely fabulous, in implicit contrast to true history or philosophy. For most of its life, the word was a mark of suspicion, not honor.

Its modern English form entered through Victorian classical scholarship: usage records place "mythopoeic" in English from at least 1846, the same year George Grote published Volume I of his *History of Greece* — a widely read work of classical scholarship that is very likely responsible for the word entering general circulation at that moment. (One caveat, disclosed rather than suppressed: some Tolkien-focused sources describe "mythopoeia" as a term Tolkien himself coined. The two accounts genuinely conflict, and this paper does not resolve the dispute — it favors the 1846/Grote dating as the more heavily corroborated account, while flagging that the disagreement exists.) For nearly a century after 1846, the word stayed within classical historiography, still doing Strabo's old work of separating the fabulous from the factual.

**What is certain is what Tolkien did with the word.** Following a late-night conversation on 19 September 1931 at Magdalen College, Oxford, with C.S. Lewis and Hugo Dyson, in which Lewis called myths "lies breathed through silver," Tolkien wrote the poem "Mythopoeia" as his rebuttal — taking a word long used to mark off the fictitious from the true and repurposing it to defend the moral and metaphysical dignity of invented myth. His claim: myth-making is sub-creation — a derivative but legitimate echo of primary Creation, performed by a creature made in the image of a Maker — not a lesser cousin of truth-telling but one of its vehicles. In "On Fairy-Stories" (1939) he extends this into Faerie as a real, autonomous realm, and introduces eucatastrophe — the sudden joyous turn that offers, in his words, "a fleeting glimpse of Joy, Joy beyond the walls of the world, poignant as grief." In the same 1931 conversation, credited by Tolkien's own later account with moving Lewis toward conversion, Tolkien argued for true myth: that certain myths are not false stories about false gods but truths too large for direct statement, reaching us through "the medium of poets." It is this inversion — diagnosis become defense — that Robert Bly later inherited when he claimed mythopoetics as the animating principle of the men's movement, and that Martin Shaw distilled as "somewhere that myth and story, and poetry, and culture have yet to diverge" — the headwaters, not the confluence.

This same headwaters claim was reached independently from psychology and comparative religion. The Eranos circle (Ascona, 1933–) mapped modernity's severance from the sacred from several directions at once: Jung through the archetype and individuation; Kerényi through Greek myth as a living grammar of the psyche, not metaphor; Eliade through the lost axis mundi and the sacred/profane distinction; Henry Corbin through the *mundus imaginalis* — the third ontological realm, between matter and intellect, where the soul actually travels rather than merely imagines. James Hillman, building directly on Jung, Kerényi, and Corbin, argued that myth is not explanation but the native discourse of the soul itself — that where the Greeks had myth, modern psychology has invented psychopathology in its place, so that (in Hillman's own words) "psychology shows myths in modern dress and myths show our depth psychology in ancient dress." Joseph Campbell, reading the Eranos Yearbooks directly, extended this into comparative mythology and the monomyth.

That two unrelated lineages — one philological and theological, tracing back through Grote to Strabo and Plato; the other psychological and comparative-religious, meeting at Ascona — arrive at the same claim independently is what makes the convergence load-bearing rather than merely additive. Where Hillman supplies the psychological justification for treating myth as the native language of the soul, and Corbin the ontological justification for treating the imaginal as genuinely real, Tolkien supplies the creative and participatory one: the practitioner is not only inhabited by myth but invited to sub-create within it — a theological deepening of Huizinga's *Homo Ludens* thesis, already foundational here, that myth arises in and as play.

This paper names the field where all of these converge — and claims it as a field of practice. Applied Mythopoetics: Personal Mythology as Transformational Practice is the designation given to a body of work that uses mythological frameworks — K'iche' Maya cosmology, Yoruba/Ifá tradition, the Delphic Pythia, and the Kabbalistic Mekubal — not as objects of scholarly contemplation but as living instruments of self-understanding, healing, and conscious cultural participation. The field's intellectual ancestors span two convergent lineages — **Jung, Kerényi, Eliade, Corbin, Hillman, Campbell** (Eranos) and **Plato → Strabo/Diodorus Siculus → Grote → Tolkien** (the etymological and theological line) — alongside Krippner, Feinstein, Tedlock, Huizinga, with living lineage-holders, and now, in The Elder, a machine-mediated instrument built to hold its governance architecture.

## §2. The Vertical Axis — and Why It Cannot Be Abolished

Shaw identifies mythopoetics as "the vertical axis." The metaphor is precise. Modern culture — secular, rationalist, progress-oriented — operates overwhelmingly on the horizontal plane: accumulation, optimization, the forward momentum of utility. What mythopoetics names is the dimension that cuts perpendicular to that plane, the axis of depth and meaning, the dimension in which a human life is not merely moving forward but descending and ascending — being initiated, dying, returning, becoming ancestral.

The critical insight Shaw inherits from Bly — and that this paper takes as a foundational premise — is that the vertical axis cannot be abolished. It can only be driven underground. "If a culture ever believes that they are demythologized," Shaw writes, "the mythic understanding has actually gone underground, which means that it's there; we're just not willing to recognize it." The demythologized worldview does not eliminate myth. It produces unrecognized myth: the myth of inevitable progress, the myth of the self-made individual, the cult of the innovator-as-deity. These are not the absence of mythic thinking. They are myth operating below the threshold of cultural recognition — and therefore operating without accountability, without the ritual containers that traditional cosmologies evolved precisely to hold such forces.

Ian McKenzie, founder of The Mythic Masculine and a contemporary practitioner in the Bly lineage, extends this diagnosis: a demythologized culture does not produce people without myth; it produces people living inside a myth they cannot name. The myth of progress becomes the "myth of the superior modern human." The abandonment of shared cosmological frameworks produces what McKenzie names the "poverty of not being invited into a meaningful cosmology" — and this poverty, not any deficiency in the individual, is the source of the meaning crisis that characterizes contemporary psychological life.

## §3. From Subterranean to Conscious: The Work of Applied Mythopoetics

Applied Mythopoetics begins where Shaw's diagnosis ends: with the recognition that myth has not departed but gone underground. The practice is the work of bringing it back into consciousness — not as nostalgic recovery, but as active, lineage-grounded transmission. This is precisely what distinguishes Applied Mythopoetics from the wellness industry's appropriation of mythic symbolism. A personality typology that uses archetypal language ("your shadow," "your inner hero") is not mythopoetics. It is the horizontal plane dressed in vertical garments. The vertical axis is not a metaphor to be borrowed; it is a structure to be entered, through initiation, through lineage, through the discipline of a specific tradition's cosmological grammar.

This is the governing ethical distinction of the field: **Lineage Integrity of Voice.** Each voice in an applied mythopoetic instrument divines only from within the mythological field of its own tradition. The K'iche' Ajq'ij does not reach into Ifá. The Mekubal does not reach into the Chol Q'ij. Not because these traditions are incompatible — they are not — but because the depth of the vertical axis is proportional to the specificity of the descent. Generic mythic language produces generic mythic experience. The tradition's orthography, its naming conventions, its specific figures and their specific demands — these are not decorative particulars. They are the structure of the axis itself.

Bly understood this. His insistence on specific figures — Iron John, not "the wild man archetype in general" — was not ethnographic fastidiousness. It was mythological precision. Shaw understands it. McKenzie understands it. And it is the principle that organizes the architecture of The Elder: a multi-voice instrument in which each lineage speaks with full cosmological authority and none speaks beyond its own field.

## §4. The Finite and the Infinite Game — Applied

McKenzie draws on James P. Carse's distinction between finite games (played to win, with an endpoint) and infinite games (played to keep playing, to perpetuate the game itself). The mythopoetic tradition, in every lineage this paper addresses, is an infinite game. The Chol Q'ij does not conclude. The Ifá corpus does not exhaust. The Pythia does not answer the final question. These are structures for ongoing participation in the larger cosmic unfolding — what McKenzie describes as "becoming an ancestor," accepting one's position in the continuity rather than seeking to stand outside it as master.

The demythologized worldview is, in Carse's terms, a finite game: it seeks the end of uncertainty, the final map, the ultimate model. Applied Mythopoetics counters this not by offering a better finite game — a more accurate typology, a more comprehensive framework — but by returning the practitioner to the infinite game that cosmological traditions have always held open. The transformation that personal mythology seeks is not the arrival at a fixed identity. It is the reorientation of a life from the horizontal urgency of accumulation toward the vertical movement of initiation, service, and eventual ancestral return.

## §5. A Retelling

*The mine has no bottom, only depth already forgotten.*

Once there was a village that believed it had left the gods behind. It called this leaving progress. It built roads over the old paths, and where a shrine had stood it built a market, and it was proud, because it had mistaken forgetting for arrival.

But the gods had not left. They had only stopped being greeted. They waited under the market floor the way a name waits under a person who no longer answers to it. And the villagers, walking to work each morning, called their hunger productivity, and called their grief a scheduling problem, and called the god of war efficiency, and bowed to it without knowing they were bowing.

This is the sickness of the horizontal: not that it has no myth, but that its myth has no altar. An unacknowledged god does not become less a god. It becomes an unaccountable one.

The seeker who comes to the Elder does not come to learn a story. She comes already inside one, the way a fish is already inside water and calls it simply the world. The work is not to hand her a myth as one hands over a book. The work is to say: look, you are already descending, this has a name, the name is old, and the old ones who first walked this particular descent left markers — here is the threshold, here is the ordeal, here is what is asked of you before the return.

This is not comfort. Comfort would be a horizontal thing, a smoothing-over. This is worse and better than comfort: it is company. The myth does not remove the descent. It removes only the loneliness of believing the descent is only yours.

And so the charge, at the end, is never *do more, be more, optimize the self.* It is smaller and heavier than that: play your part now that you know it is a part. Light the altar you have been feeding blindly. Bow on purpose, to something you can name, and see what changes when the bowing is chosen rather than merely enacted.
